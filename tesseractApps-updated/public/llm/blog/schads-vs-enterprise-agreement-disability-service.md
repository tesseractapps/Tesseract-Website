# SCHADS vs Enterprise Agreement: Which Is Better for Your Disability Service?

Difference between SCHADS and  enterprise agreement in the Australian disability and community services sector with 2026 award changes in play.

If you run an NDIS or community services organisation, you have probably had this conversation at least once in the boardroom: should we stay on SCHADS, or is it time to negotiate our own enterprise agreement?

It is a fair question, and a timely one. The Fair Work Commission's June 2026 decision to overhaul SCHADS -a new classification structure, a wage reset, and the removal of the Equal Remuneration Order - has put award coverage back on the agenda for boards that had not touched their industrial instrument in years. With an interim pay rise for disability home care workers from October 2026, and a full restructure a year later, the question of which instrument to be on is now a budgeting question, not a theoretical one.

This article breaks down what SCHADS and an enterprise agreement actually offer disability providers, where each option helps or hurts, and the BOOT test traps that catch even experienced HR teams out.

## Quick refresher: what is the SCHADS Award?

The Social, Community, Home Care and Disability Services Industry Award sets [minimum pay and conditions](https://tesseractapps.com.au/blog/schads-award-2026-the-complete-guide-for-ndis-and-disability-providers) for most disability support, home care, and community services workers in Australia. It is a modern award, meaning it is a safety net set centrally by the Fair Work Commission, not something your organisation negotiated.

SCHADS has long been criticised for being complex and inconsistent across its schedules, which is exactly what the Commission's 2026 gender-based undervaluation review set out to fix. The upshot: a new integrated classification structure is coming, home care and disability schedule employees get an interim increase from 1 October 2026, and the rest of the workforce transitions under the Final Classification Structure from 1 October 2027. Existing employees keep their current pay during translation, but the compliance workload of remapping every role is real.

## What is an enterprise agreement, and why do providers consider one?

An enterprise agreement, sometimes called an EBA, is a registered agreement negotiated directly between an employer and its employees, often with union involvement, covering wages and conditions in place of the award. Once employees vote to approve it, the employer lodges it with the Fair Work Commission for formal approval.

Providers move to an EBA to simplify a workforce that has outgrown award classifications, build in [rostering flexibility](https://tesseractapps.com.au/blog/how-ndis-providers-track-employee-hours-accurately) for 24/7 disability support work, trade off penalty rates for higher base pay, or gain more predictable and budgetable labour costs than an award that reshapes itself every few years. For some providers, a well-built NDIS enterprise agreement is the practical SCHADS alternative that finally matches how their rosters actually run.

## SCHADS vs enterprise agreement: the honest comparison

### Staying on SCHADS

**Pros:** No bargaining cost or timeline. Automatic compliance with award updates. Familiar to staff and easy to benchmark. No BOOT test exposure, since award minimums are already the floor you are paying to.

**Cons:** Limited flexibility on rostering and shift design. Minimal room to trade off conditions for operational needs. Right now, a period of genuine uncertainty as the new classification structure phases in over 2026 to 2027.

### Moving to an enterprise agreement

**Pros:** Tailored rostering and shift patterns that fit disability support delivery. Potential to simplify classifications for a large or mixed workforce. Greater cost predictability once locked in. An opportunity to modernise conditions that SCHADS was not designed for.

**Cons:** A genuine bargaining process with real time, cost, and industrial relations effort. A legal requirement to pass the BOOT test for every classification and roster pattern. Ongoing risk if your agreement falls behind future award increases. A real prospect of employee or union pushback during negotiation.

Neither option is universally better. A boutique provider with 20 support workers on fairly standard rosters may get little value from the cost and complexity of bargaining. A large provider running complex [24/7 rosters](https://tesseractapps.com.au/blog/schads-sleepover-active-hours-travel) across multiple sites, with genuine flexibility needs, is far more likely to benefit from a tailored agreement - provided it is built and tested properly.

## The BOOT test: where enterprise agreements go wrong

The Better Off Overall Test is the single biggest hurdle to getting an enterprise agreement approved, and the single biggest source of failed applications in the disability and community services sector. The Fair Work Commission must be satisfied that every employee, in every classification and roster pattern the agreement covers, would be better off overall under the agreement than under the award.

The traps that catch providers out most often:

1. **Testing against today's SCHADS, not tomorrow's.** With the award mid-restructure, an agreement that clears the BOOT against current rates can fail against the new classification structure and wage rates rolling out from October 2026 and October 2027. Agreements need to be modelled against where the award is heading, not just where it sits today.
2. **Averaging across a group instead of testing each employee.** The BOOT is assessed employee by employee, or by representative work patterns, not as an average saving across the workforce. A roster pattern that is fine for day shift workers can fail for night shift or sleepover employees.
3. **Ignoring allowances and penalties in the trade-off.** Base rate increases that look generous on paper can still fail the BOOT once travel time, [sleepover provisions](https://tesseractapps.com.au/blog/schads-sleepover-active-hours-travel), overtime, and allowances are properly accounted for. These are areas SCHADS treats in detail and that are easy to underweight in bargaining.
4. **Locking in terms without a wage review mechanism.** An agreement with no built-in increases can start compliant and quietly fall behind the award within a couple of wage reviews, creating a live BOOT problem later in the agreement's life, not just at approval. A wage review mechanism is essential, not optional.
5. **Underestimating sleepover and overnight shift complexity.** Following the Federal Court's ruling in the Jats Joint case, the Fair Work Commission's final determination confirmed that from 1 June 2026, a sleepover no longer counts as a break under SCHADS. Work performed either side of it is treated as one continuous shift. Any agreement covering overnight disability support needs sleepover and shift-loading provisions modelled against this current position, not a generic clause copied from another sector.

## Is now the right time to make the move?

Given the scale of the 2026 to 2027 SCHADS restructure, timing matters more than usual. Bargaining an agreement mid-overhaul without modelling the new classification structure is a good way to end up back at the Commission for a variation within 18 months. Waiting until October 2027 to even start the conversation, though, may leave cost and flexibility benefits on the table for a long transition period.

For most boards, the sensible sequence is: map your workforce against the incoming SCHADS classifications, model the [cost and compliance impact](https://tesseractapps.com.au/blog/how-to-manage-compliance-risk-ndis-providers), then decide whether an enterprise agreement justifies the bargaining effort -built to withstand the award as it will look in 2027, not just as it looks today.

## Which option is right for your organisation?

There is no one-size-fits-all answer, and providers who get burned usually picked a side before doing the modelling. The right call depends on your workforce size, roster complexity, growth plans, and appetite for an industrial relations process. What is consistent across the sector: getting the BOOT test wrong, or ignoring where SCHADS is heading, is an expensive mistake to unwind.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is an enterprise agreement always cheaper than SCHADS?

Not necessarily. An EBA can lower some costs, like penalty rates, while raising others, like base pay, and it must pass the BOOT test regardless. The real driver of value is usually rostering flexibility, not a straight wage discount.

### Can we move from SCHADS to an enterprise agreement at any time?

Yes, there is no fixed window, but bargaining takes time and must run in good faith. Given the current SCHADS restructure, most compliance advisors recommend modelling against the incoming award changes before locking in agreement terms.

### Does an enterprise agreement remove SCHADS coverage entirely?

Yes. Once an enterprise agreement is approved and in operation, it applies instead of the award for covered employees, though the award can still be relevant for interpreting entitlements not addressed in the agreement.

### What happens if our enterprise agreement fails the BOOT test later on?

The BOOT is assessed at approval, not continuously. But if [award increases](https://tesseractapps.com.au/blog/schads-overtime-penalty-rates) later overtake your agreed rates, the agreement does not automatically become non-compliant; it becomes vulnerable to a variation application, a termination application, or pressure to renegotiate at the next bargaining round. This is why a built-in wage review or increase mechanism is essential, not optional.
