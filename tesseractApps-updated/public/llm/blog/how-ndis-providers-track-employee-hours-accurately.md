# How NDIS Providers Can Track Employee Hours Accurately (and Stay Compliant)

Inaccurate work hour tracking is one of the most common and most expensive problems in NDIS workforce management. Here's how to fix it properly.

Tracking employee work hours sounds straightforward. A worker starts a shift, finishes a shift, and someone records the hours. Simple.

Except in an NDIS context, it is not. The hours a worker is present matter less than the hours they are performing specific supports, with specific participants, under specific funding lines. A four-hour shift might contain thirty minutes of community access, two hours of personal care, and ninety minutes of household tasks, each potentially billed differently, each potentially requiring separate documentation.

Add [SCHADS Award](/capabilities/timesheets-payroll-alignment) complexity including [penalty rates](/ndis-glossary), allowances, broken shift provisions, and sleepover conditions, and tracking hours accurately becomes a compliance-critical function, not a simple HR task.

When it goes wrong, it goes wrong in two directions: workers are underpaid, or participants are overbilled. Neither is acceptable, and both carry consequences.

## The Most Common Ways NDIS Providers Get This Wrong

### Paper timesheets and manual entry

Paper is the highest-risk timesheet format in existence. It cannot be timestamped, it cannot be validated against the roster, it can be written retroactively, and it has to be manually transcribed into payroll. Every step in that chain is an opportunity for error.

### Workers self-reporting without verification

When workers submit their own hours without any cross-check against the rostered shift, no GPS verification, no supervisor confirmation, no system match, there is no mechanism to catch discrepancies. Small overstatements across a large workforce add up to significant payroll leakage.

### Rostered hours used as actual hours

Some providers pay based on rostered shifts rather than actual time worked. For shifts that run predictably, this might be fine. For a workforce where shift lengths vary, participants cancel, or workers finish early or late, it is a systematic inaccuracy.

### No differentiation by support type

If all hours in a shift are recorded as a single block, there is no way to correctly attribute costs to funding lines, bill the right NDIS support categories, or produce accurate reports for participants or plan managers. This directly affects invoicing accuracy.

> Paying from the roster instead of actual hours is not an efficiency. It is a liability. Either workers are underpaid, or participants are overbilled. Usually both.

## What Accurate Hour Tracking Actually Requires

Fixing the problem properly means building the right controls into the workflow, not adding more manual steps at the end.

### Clock-in and clock-out linked to the shift record

Workers should [clock in and out digitally](/capabilities/clock-in-out), with their recorded times automatically matched against the rostered shift. Discrepancies should surface for supervisor review, not silently pass through to payroll.

### GPS or location verification for community-based shifts

For workers delivering supports in participants homes or in the community, location-based verification provides a basic check that the worker was where they were supposed to be. This is about having an auditable record that the support was delivered.

### Support-type tagging within shifts

The platform should allow workers to log what they were doing during a shift, not just that they were present. This creates the data needed to accurately attribute hours to the right NDIS support categories.

### Supervisor approval before payroll

No hours should reach payroll without a supervisor review step. The supervisor should be able to see the rostered shift, the clocked hours, and any flagged discrepancies, and approve, reject, or amend before the data flows downstream.

### A locked record after approval

Once hours are approved and paid, the record should be immutable. Changes to historical records should require a formal amendment process with an audit trail, not a simple edit.

## The HR and Payroll Risk of Getting This Wrong

The Fair Work Act and the [SCHADS Award](/capabilities/timesheets-payroll-alignment) both carry significant obligations around accurate payment. Underpayment, even unintentional, can result in back-payment orders, penalties, and reputational damage. The Fair Work Ombudsman recovered over $532 million in underpaid wages in 2022-23, with the care and community services sector among the most investigated industries.

Beyond legal risk, inaccurate hours tracking creates internal friction. Workers who consistently receive incorrect pay do not stay. And from the NDIS billing side, hours that do not match delivered supports create audit risk. If your claimed hours do not reconcile with your timesheet records, you have a compliance exposure that no amount of good intention will fix in a Commission review.

## What Good Looks Like in Practice

The right hour-tracking workflow looks like this, regardless of the system you use:

1. Workers clock in digitally at shift start, with a location check for community-based supports
2. Clock-out is recorded digitally at shift end, matched automatically against the roster
3. Support types are tagged during or immediately after the shift
4. Any discrepancy between clocked and rostered hours is flagged to a supervisor
5. The supervisor reviews and approves or queries before the record is locked
6. Approved records flow directly to payroll with the correct [SCHADS rates](/capabilities/timesheets-payroll-alignment) applied
7. The same confirmed record generates the [participant invoice](/capabilities/ndis-claiming-invoicing)
