# Minimum Engagement Periods Under SCHADS: The 2-Hour Rule and Common Pitfalls

What counts toward minimum engagement under SCHADS, how breaks affect it, and the pitfalls Fair Work auditors flag most often.

Of all the ways NDIS providers end up with an underpayment liability, missing minimum engagement periods is one of the most avoidable. The rule is straightforward, the dollar value on any single shift is small, and that combination is exactly why it accumulates quietly for years before anyone notices. The Fair Work Ombudsman recovered more than $30 million in underpayments from the care sector in 2024-25. Minimum engagement was cited as one of the most frequent causes alongside misclassification and broken shift allowances. World Vision Australia repaid over $6 million in June 2024. Open Minds Australia paid $4.2 million in May 2024. These were not small operators with manual payroll and no HR function. They were established organisations.

Since January 2025, intentional underpayment is a criminal offence. A payroll configuration someone knowingly chose, even years ago, is potentially in scope. This guide covers exactly how the SCHADS minimum engagement rules work under clause 10.5, which employees they apply to, how breaks affect them, and where Fair Work auditors find the gaps.

## What the award actually says?

Clause 10.5 of the SCHADS Award sets minimum payment obligations for part-time and casual employees. It is not a general rule about shift length for everyone under the award. It applies specifically to two employment types: part-time and casual. Full-time employees are governed by their contracted hours and the ordinary hours provisions, not clause 10.5.

Within those two employment types, the minimum varies by stream and role:

**Three hours per shift** applies to social and community services employees, except when they are performing disability services work.

**Two hours per shift** applies to all other employees under the award: home care employees, crisis accommodation employees, family day care employees, and SACS employees who are performing disability services work.

That distinction between SACS-non-disability and SACS-disability is one of the most commonly misread rules in the award. A support coordinator doing non-disability case management is on a three-hour minimum. A SACS employee delivering direct disability support is on two hours. Applying the wrong minimum to the wrong role is an error that repeats every pay cycle.

## What "minimum engagement" actually means in practice?

The minimum engagement period is the minimum amount of time a part-time or casual employee must be paid for each shift or work period, regardless of how long they actually worked.

A casual support worker rostered for a one-hour community access visit must be paid for two hours at the applicable rate, including the casual loading and any penalty rates for the day or time. A part-time SACS employee rostered for a two-hour wellness check must be paid for three hours if they are doing non-disability work. The worker leaving after 45 minutes, or the visit running short because the participant cancelled partway through, does not reduce the minimum payment obligation.

This is the part that surprises most rostering coordinators. The minimum is not a floor on the shift as scheduled. It is a floor on the payment, regardless of what actually happens on the day.

## How minimum engagement interacts with broken shifts?

Each work period within a broken shift must independently meet the minimum engagement requirement. A broken shift is not assessed as a whole against the daily minimum. It is assessed period by period.

A home care worker rostered for a 90-minute morning visit and a 90-minute afternoon visit with an unpaid break in between has two work periods that each fall below the two-hour minimum. Both must be paid as two hours. The shift costs the employer four hours of pay, plus the broken shift allowance, for three hours of actual work. A roster built around 90-minute visits across a split day is systematically underpaying unless it is calculating each period against the minimum.

This is one of the most common patterns CrossVault and other SCHADS audit tools flag in timesheet reviews: visits of 60 to 90 minutes paired into broken shifts, with payroll calculating actual time rather than minimum time for each period.

## Client cancellations and the minimum engagement obligation

Under clause 25.5, if a client cancels with less than seven days notice, the employer can redirect the worker to other duties. If redirection is not possible, the employer may cancel the shift but must pay the employee the amount they would have received, or provide make-up time within six weeks. For same-day cancellations, the minimum engagement obligation applies the moment the worker presents for duty. A worker who arrives at a participant's home to find the service cancelled must still be paid for the minimum period. The obligation does not disappear because the participant was unavailable.

## The Woolworths ruling and why each pay period matters

The Federal Court's September 2025 decision in _Fair Work Ombudsman v Woolworths Group Limited_ [2025] FCA 1092 confirmed that overpayments in one pay period cannot offset underpayments in another, and each entitlement must be satisfied separately. A roster that overpays some workers on some shifts gives no credit against minimum engagement shortfalls elsewhere. The shortfalls accumulate across the six-year period the Fair Work Ombudsman can examine.

## The most common minimum engagement errors

**Calculating actual time instead of minimum time.** The most basic error, and the most widespread. Payroll systems that record actual clock-in and clock-out times and pay on the minute have no mechanism to apply the two or three-hour floor unless that rule has been explicitly configured. A system paying 73 minutes for a 73-minute visit is not compliant. Someone has to have set up the minimum engagement top-up, or it does not apply.

**Applying the wrong minimum to the role.** Three hours for non-disability SACS, two hours for everyone else. The error usually runs in one direction: applying two hours to SACS employees doing non-disability coordination work where three hours should apply. Less commonly, three hours gets applied to disability support workers where two is correct. Both are wrong.

**Excluding casuals from the minimum.** Some providers operate on the assumption that casuals, because they are paid per shift with a loading, are not covered by minimum engagement. They are. The casual loading compensates for the absence of paid leave. It does not substitute for the minimum engagement obligation. A casual on a 45-minute shift must still be paid for two hours at their full casual rate including penalty rates for the time of day.

**Missing the per-period rule in broken shifts.** As above: each work period in a broken shift carries its own minimum. A shift with two periods of 75 minutes each is two separate underpayments, not one shift that totals 150 minutes.

**Treating cancellation as cancellation of the obligation.** If a worker presents for duty and the service is cancelled, the minimum engagement clock has already started.

## What Fair Work auditors actually look for?

The Fair Work Ombudsman's compliance approach has shifted toward data-led auditing. Pattern detection flags providers whose payroll shows a concentration of shifts paid at exactly the visit duration: 60 minutes, 90 minutes, 120 minutes. A roster of 90-minute visits with 90-minute payments across a casual workforce surfaces quickly. A single complaint can open a full audit covering all employees, with records examinable back seven years. Penalties reach $93,900 per contravention for a small business employer and $939,000 for serious contraventions. With minimum engagement applying to every work period of every affected employee, systemic errors produce a large number of contraventions fast.

## Minimum engagement under SCHADS FAQ

**What is the minimum engagement period under SCHADS?** 

Two hours per shift or work period for most employees, including home care workers, disability support workers, crisis accommodation staff and SACS employees doing disability services work. Three hours per shift for SACS employees performing non-disability social and community services work.

**Does the minimum engagement apply to casual employees?** 

Yes. The two-hour and three-hour minimums apply to both part-time and casual employees under clause 10.5 of the SCHADS Award. The casual loading does not substitute for the minimum engagement obligation.

**Does the minimum engagement apply per broken shift or per work period?** Per work period. Each separate period of work within a broken shift must independently meet the two-hour or three-hour minimum.

**What happens if a client cancels and the worker has already arrived?** 

The minimum engagement obligation applies from the moment the worker presents for duty. A same-day cancellation does not remove the obligation to pay the minimum engagement period. The employer must pay the applicable minimum or provide make-up time within six weeks, depending on the circumstances.

**Can overpayments on other shifts offset minimum engagement shortfalls?** No. Following the Federal Court's 2025 decision in FWO v Woolworths, overpayments in one pay period cannot offset underpayments in another, and each entitlement must be satisfied separately.

**How far back can Fair Work audit minimum engagement underpayments?** Seven years under the Fair Work Act, though the standard limitation period for court proceedings is six years. A single complaint can trigger an audit covering all employees, not just the complainant.
