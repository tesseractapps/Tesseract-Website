# Top 7 SCHADS Award Breaches Found by Fair Work, and How Software Prevents Them

The seven SCHADS breaches Fair Work most often penalises Australian providers for, with real case examples, and the automated controls that stop them at source.

The Fair Work Ombudsman has made disability and aged care a priority enforcement area, and the numbers back it up. In the 2024 to 2025 financial year alone, the regulator recovered more than $30 million in underpayments from the care sector. Since January 2025, intentional underpayment is also a criminal offence: companies face the greater of $7.825 million or three times the underpayment amount, and individuals found guilty face up to 10 years' imprisonment.

None of that is meant to alarm you. Most SCHADS breaches at NDIS and disability providers are not deliberate. They come from award interpretation errors, spreadsheets that cannot keep up with more than 3,000 possible pay rate combinations, and payroll systems that were not built for a modern award this complex. Below are the seven SCHADS award breaches Fair Work most consistently finds, with real cases behind each one, and what actually stops them before they reach payroll.

## 1. Sleepover and night shift loading errors

[Sleepovers are the single most litigated area of the SCHADS Award](https://tesseractapps.com.au/blog/schads-sleepover-active-hours-travel) right now. For years the Fair Work Ombudsman held that work performed either side of a sleepover formed one continuous shift, triggering a 15% night shift loading. That position was tested in Jats Joint Pty Ltd v Fair Work Ombudsman [2025] FCA 743, where the Federal Court found in the employer's favour and ruled that a sleepover does not form part of a shift. The Full Federal Court then dismissed the Ombudsman's appeal in March 2026.

The Fair Work Commission has since settled the question by varying the award itself. From the first full pay period on or after 1 June 2026, sleepovers officially do not count as a break, work either side is treated as one continuous shift, and shift penalties are now calculated separately for each active work period rather than across the whole engagement. Providers running 24-hour care models are now managing three different rule sets depending on the pay period in question: pre-Jats Joint, post-Jats Joint, and post-June 2026 determination.

**How software prevents it:** Rostering and payroll platforms with SCHADS interpretation built in apply the correct sleepover, shift, and penalty logic automatically based on the pay period date, rather than relying on payroll staff to track which legal position applied when.

## 2. Applying the wrong award or classification stream

[SCHADS has four streams and up to eight classification levels](https://tesseractapps.com.au/blog/schads-award-2026-the-complete-guide-for-ndis-and-disability-providers), and the gap between them is material. A Level 2 worker in the Home Care and Disability stream and a Level 2 worker in the Social and Community Services stream sit on different base rates entirely. Some providers compound the problem by classifying support workers under the Home Care Award instead of SCHADS altogether, a red flag that industry bodies including the Australian Services Union actively watch for.

Get the stream or level wrong on day one and every subsequent pay run inherits the error, often for years before anyone notices.

**How software prevents it:** Classification logic tied to role, funding stream, and duties, checked at onboarding and flagged automatically if a role looks misclassified against its actual work pattern.

## 3. Missed travel, transport, and fare allowances

The Fair Work Ombudsman issued a Compliance Notice to disability provider Agape in October 2024 after two employees, one casual and one full-time, were found to have been underpaid between November 2023 and May 2024. The alleged underpayments totalled $7,789.42, driven by the casual worker's minimum hourly rate and travelling, transport, and fare allowances under SCHADS, and the full-time worker's annual leave entitlements under the National Employment Standards.

[Travel time and allowances](https://tesseractapps.com.au/blog/how-ndis-providers-track-employee-hours-accurately) are a recurring blind spot precisely because they depend on shift-by-shift detail, client location, and mode of travel, none of which a static pay rate captures.

**How software prevents it:** Allowances calculated from actual rostered travel and shift data, not applied as a flat rate or left to manual timesheet entry.

## 4. National Employment Standards entitlements missed alongside the award

The Agape matter is a useful reminder that SCHADS breaches rarely travel alone. The same investigation that flagged the casual worker's allowance shortfall also found the full-time employee had been underpaid annual leave entitlements under the [National Employment Standards (NES](https://tesseractapps.com.au/blog/schads-vs-enterprise-agreement-disability-service)), a separate but equally enforceable set of minimum standards that sits alongside the award.

Providers often build strong award compliance while treating NES entitlements like leave accrual as a background payroll function, which is exactly where gaps open up.

**How software prevents it:** Leave accrual, entitlement balances, and award interpretation running in the same system, so NES and SCHADS obligations are checked together rather than in separate processes.

## 5. Broken shift and minimum engagement breaches

[Broken shifts](https://tesseractapps.com.au/blog/schads-broken-shifts-rostering-without-breaches), where an employee's working day is split by an unpaid gap, and [minimum engagement periods for casuals](https://tesseractapps.com.au/blog/schads-minimum-engagement) are two of the more technical corners of SCHADS, and among the easiest to get wrong manually. A single miscalculated broken shift allowance or a casual engaged under the two-hour minimum does not look dramatic on one payslip, but compounds fast across a large casual workforce running hundreds of short client visits a week.

**How software prevents it:** Shift structures validated against SCHADS minimum engagement and broken shift rules at the rostering stage, before the shift is even worked, not after payroll has already run.

## 6. Missing the annual wage review

SCHADS rates increase from the first full pay period on or after 1 July each year, following the Fair Work Commission's Annual Wage Review. Rates rose 3.5% from 1 July 2025, and a further 4.75% increase applies from 1 July 2026. Providers that update rates manually, or rely on someone remembering to check the Fair Work Ombudsman's pay guide, routinely run at least one full pay cycle at the old rate. Multiply that gap across a large workforce and it becomes a genuine underpayment, not just an administrative delay.

**How software prevents it:** Award rates that update centrally the moment the Fair Work Commission publishes new figures, so every future pay run reflects the correct rate without anyone needing to manually re-key it.

## 7. Record-keeping gaps that turn a small error into a big one

The Fair Work Ombudsman requires [payroll records, timesheets, and payslips](https://tesseractapps.com.au/blog/schads-award-audit-guide-self-assess-fair-work) to be retained for at least seven years. Manual systems and spreadsheets rarely produce clean, audit-ready records over that timeframe, which matters enormously if a breach is ever found. It was self-reporting and a clear audit trail that allowed disability and community services provider Uniting Communities Incorporated to enter an Enforceable Undertaking with the Fair Work Ombudsman after identifying underpayments spanning November 2015 to December 2025 across its 65 South Australian sites. The organisation is returning more than $2.6 million, including superannuation and interest, to around 1,500 current and former employees.

That case is also a reminder of the scale that small, unnoticed errors can reach over a decade of manual or semi-manual payroll. Early self-reporting worked in Uniting Communities' favour, but the underlying issue, a decade of compounding award interpretation errors, is exactly what automated controls are designed to catch far earlier.

**How software prevents it:** Every shift, rate, and allowance calculation logged automatically, with a complete audit-ready history retained for the full statutory period, so a Fair Work investigation is a data export rather than a scramble.

## The pattern behind all seven

Every case above shares the same root cause: SCHADS is too complex, and changes too often, for manual processes or generic payroll software to track reliably. Sleepover rules alone have shifted three times in under twelve months. Rates change annually. Allowances depend on shift-level detail. None of that is a criticism of payroll or rostering teams. It is simply more variables than a spreadsheet was ever built to hold consistently correct.

Purpose-built SCHADS interpretation software closes that gap by encoding the award's rules directly into rostering and payroll, so compliance happens automatically at the point a shift is built, not months later when Fair Work comes asking questions.
