# NDIS Pricing 2026–27: What Providers Should Check in Their Systems

The 2026–27 NDIS pricing schedule is now in effect. See what providers should review across agreements, systems, billing, claiming and workforce costs.

The **NDIS Pricing Schedule for 2026–27 took effect on 1 July 2026**, bringing another annual pricing update for participants and providers.

For NDIS providers, a pricing update is not just something for the finance team to review.

It can affect service agreements, support items, rostering assumptions, invoicing, claiming, workforce costs and the information stored across your operational systems.

The NDIA says providers can use the 2026–27 Pricing Schedule to inform their prices. Importantly, any proposed change to pricing in an existing service agreement needs to be discussed with the participant and agreed before the change is made.

So after a new pricing schedule is released, simply updating a number in your software may not be enough.

Here are the areas providers should check.

## 1. Make sure your team is using the current NDIS Pricing Schedule

This sounds obvious, but pricing information often ends up in multiple places.

One copy may be stored in the finance team's folder.

Another may sit in a spreadsheet used by administration.

Someone else may have downloaded an older PDF.

The current **2026–27 NDIS Pricing Schedule is effective from 1 July 2026**.

Providers should first identify which support items are relevant to the services they actually deliver.

Depending on your organisation, review:

- support item numbers
- support item descriptions
- units
- applicable pricing
- remote and very remote arrangements where relevant
- any conditions associated with the support
- effective dates

You do not necessarily need to review every item in the entire Pricing Schedule.

Start with the services your organisation delivers and the support items your teams actually use.

## 2. Don't automatically change existing participant prices

This is an important distinction.

The NDIS Pricing Schedule provides guidance about appropriate prices, but a new schedule does not automatically rewrite an existing agreement between a provider and participant.

The NDIA's current guidance says providers must discuss proposed changes to existing service agreements with participants, and participants must agree before those changes are made.

That means your pricing-change workflow may need to include more than:

**New schedule released → update system**

It may instead look like:

**Pricing change identified → affected participants identified → agreement reviewed → participant contacted → change agreed → effective date recorded → system updated**

Having that process clearly documented can help reduce confusion later.

## 3. Check which service agreements need attention

Not every participant or service will necessarily be affected in the same way.

Providers should be able to identify:

- which participants use affected support items
- what price is currently agreed
- whether an agreement permits or requires a pricing review
- whether a participant has agreed to a proposed change
- when the new price becomes effective
- who recorded or approved the change

This is where disconnected systems can create problems.

If participant agreements live in one system, pricing in another and invoicing somewhere else, it can become difficult to know whether all three still match.

## 4. Review the pricing configuration inside your software

Once the commercial and participant-agreement side has been confirmed, review your operational systems.

Check whether:

- current support items are available
- obsolete information remains active
- prices have appropriate effective dates
- participant-specific arrangements are preserved
- historical services retain historical pricing
- remote or very remote arrangements are configured correctly where relevant
- staff know who is authorised to change pricing information

One of the biggest risks with system changes is updating a default value without understanding what else depends on it.

For example, changing the default price for a support item should not accidentally alter the historical record for a service delivered before the change took effect.

## 5. Test the entire service-to-claim workflow

After changing pricing information, do not only check the pricing screen.

Run a test through the actual operational process.

For example:

**Roster → service delivered → timesheet → invoice → claim preparation → reconciliation**

Check whether the correct information flows through each stage.

Questions worth testing include:

- Did the correct support item appear?
- Was the correct price applied?
- Was the correct date used?
- Does the invoice match the service delivered?
- Did any manual override remain from the previous pricing period?
- Does the claim information match the invoice?
- Can the finance team trace the amount back to the original service?

Testing the complete workflow can reveal problems that are invisible when looking only at the rate table.

## 6. Look at workforce costs as well as NDIS prices

Pricing is only one side of provider sustainability.

The NDIA has also released the **NDIS Disability Support Worker Cost Model 2026–27**, effective from 1 July 2026. The model estimates the cost of delivering a billable hour of support.

The 2026–27 Annual Pricing Review also considered wage movements under the SCHADS Award when assessing prices linked to the Disability Support Worker Cost Model.

Providers therefore need to consider both:

**What can we charge?**

and

**What does it actually cost us to deliver the support?**

Operational costs may include:

- wages
- superannuation
- leave
- overtime
- training
- supervision
- travel
- non-billable administration
- cancellations
- software
- insurance
- compliance
- management overheads

A higher support price does not automatically mean the provider has a higher margin.

Understanding the cost of delivering the service is just as important.

## 7. Review rostering assumptions

Pricing and workforce operations are closely connected.

A roster determines more than who works where.

It can affect:

- labour costs
- travel
- overtime
- shift allocation
- utilisation
- cancellations
- billable hours
- payroll
- service profitability

If pricing changes but workforce assumptions remain outdated, management may still have an incomplete picture of whether a particular service is sustainable.

This is why pricing should not sit entirely inside the finance function.

Operations, rostering and finance should all understand how the updated arrangements affect the organisation.

## 8. Check for manual spreadsheets and duplicated pricing tables

Many provider organisations gradually accumulate spreadsheets.

A spreadsheet may have originally been created for one simple purpose, but over time it becomes an unofficial source of truth.

After a pricing update, check whether staff still use:

- old pricing spreadsheets
- downloaded support catalogues
- locally saved PDFs
- manually created billing sheets
- personal templates
- old service-agreement templates

If several versions of pricing information exist, someone will eventually use the wrong one.

Where possible, providers should establish a clear source of current information and communicate where staff should find it.

## 9. Check your invoicing workflow

Pricing problems sometimes become visible only when invoices are created.

Test invoices from several realistic scenarios.

For example:

- a standard weekday service
- a participant with an individually agreed price
- a service delivered before a price change
- a service delivered after a price change
- remote or very remote services where relevant
- services that involve different support items

The aim is to make sure the invoice reflects what was actually agreed and delivered.

If staff regularly need to manually correct invoices after they are generated, that may indicate a deeper workflow or configuration problem.

## 10. Review unsuccessful or rejected claims

Pricing updates are also a useful time to review your claim-management process.

When a claim does not proceed as expected, can your team easily determine why?

Ideally, staff should be able to trace the claim back through:

**Claim → invoice → delivered service → roster/service record → participant agreement**

Without that visibility, finance teams may spend unnecessary time investigating the same issue across multiple systems.

Over time, rejected or unsuccessful claims can also provide useful operational information.

If the same types of issues repeatedly occur, the underlying problem may be in:

- service setup
- data entry
- pricing configuration
- participant funding information
- support-item selection
- staff processes
- system integration

The objective should not only be to correct the individual claim.

It should also be to understand why the problem occurred.

## 11. Keep a record of pricing changes

Providers should consider maintaining a clear history of important changes.

Depending on your processes, this could include:

- date the pricing review was completed
- affected support items
- affected participants
- participant communication
- agreement changes
- approval records
- system configuration changes
- effective dates
- staff notifications
- testing results

A good audit trail makes it easier to answer questions later.

For example:

**Why was this participant charged this amount on this date?**

If the answer requires searching through emails, spreadsheets and old documents, there may be an opportunity to improve the workflow.

## 12. Make sure operational teams understand the update

Pricing should not be treated as finance-only information.

Depending on your provider model, people who may need to understand part of the change include:

- owners and directors
- operations managers
- service managers
- finance teams
- rostering teams
- administrators
- support coordinators
- relevant frontline staff

They do not all need to understand every line of the NDIS Pricing Schedule.

But they should understand the parts that affect their responsibilities.

For example, a scheduler may need to understand how the service configuration affects the roster without needing to manage the claiming process.

Clear responsibilities help reduce accidental changes and inconsistent information.

## A practical 2026–27 NDIS pricing systems checklist

Use this checklist to review your organisation:

- We are using the current 2026–27 NDIS Pricing Schedule
- Relevant support items have been reviewed
- Existing service agreements have been checked
- Proposed pricing changes have been discussed and agreed where required
- Effective dates are recorded
- Pricing inside our software has been reviewed
- Historical pricing has not been unintentionally changed
- Participant-specific arrangements remain correct
- Rostering and service-delivery workflows have been tested
- Invoices have been tested
- Claim workflows have been tested
- Workforce-cost assumptions have been reviewed
- Old spreadsheets and pricing documents have been identified
- Staff know where to find current information
- Pricing changes have an appropriate record or audit trail

## Pricing changes are really workflow changes

It is easy to think of an NDIS pricing update as a finance exercise.

But in practice, pricing can connect multiple parts of a provider organisation:

**Participant agreement → service configuration → roster → service delivery → timesheet → invoice → claim → payment → reporting**

If those stages are disconnected, every pricing change can create additional administration.

If they are connected, it becomes easier to understand which price applies, what was delivered, what was billed and why.

This is one reason providers should consider the entire operational workflow when reviewing their technology—not only individual features.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When did the 2026–27 NDIS Pricing Schedule take effect?

The current 2026–27 NDIS Pricing Schedule took effect on **1 July 2026**.

### Can NDIS providers automatically increase prices when the Pricing Schedule changes?

Providers can use the NDIS Pricing Schedule to inform their pricing, but the NDIA states that proposed changes to existing service agreements must be discussed with participants and agreed before they are made.

### What is the NDIS Disability Support Worker Cost Model?

The Disability Support Worker Cost Model estimates the cost of delivering a billable hour of support. The 2026–27 version is effective from 1 July 2026.

### Should providers update their software after an NDIS pricing change?

Providers should review any system configuration affected by the new pricing arrangements, but changes should be made carefully. Existing agreements, effective dates, historical pricing and participant-specific arrangements should also be considered.

### Where can providers find the current NDIS Pricing Schedule?

The current Pricing Schedule and related pricing documents are published on the official NDIS website.
