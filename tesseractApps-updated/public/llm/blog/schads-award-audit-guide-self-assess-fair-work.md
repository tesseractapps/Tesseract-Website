# The Ultimate SCHADS Award Audit Guide: Self-Assess Before Fair Work Knocks

A practical SCHADS Audit guide for NDIS providers: sample audit queries, evidence pack structure, and the 7 highest-risk clauses Fair Work investigates first.

Disability and aged care is now a declared priority enforcement area for the Fair Work Ombudsman. In the 2024 to 2025 financial year, the regulator recovered more than $30 million in underpayments from the care sector, and since January 2025, intentional underpayment carries criminal penalties: the greater of $7.825 million or three times the underpayment amount for a company, and up to 10 years' imprisonment for individuals found guilty.

That is the backdrop every NDIS provider now operates in. The good news is that a genuine self-audit, done properly and before a regulator asks for one, is one of the few compliance activities that actually changes your outcome if something is wrong. This guide walks Directors and Compliance Leads through how Fair Work actually investigates, the seven SCHADS clauses that generate the most enforcement activity, sample queries to run against your own payroll data, and how to build an evidence pack that stands up to scrutiny.

## How Fair Work actually audits providers?

**Compliance notices.** Where the FWO has reasonable grounds to believe a contravention occurred, it can issue a compliance notice requiring the employer to calculate and rectify the underpayment within a set timeframe. This is typically how smaller, contained matters begin, as seen when the FWO issued a compliance notice to disability provider Agape in October 2024 over a $7,789.42 underpayment involving award allowances and NES leave entitlements.

**Enforceable Undertakings.** For larger or systemic issues, particularly where an employer self-reports, the FWO can accept an Enforceable Undertaking in place of court action. Disability and community services provider Uniting Communities Incorporated entered an Enforceable Undertaking after self-reporting underpayments spanning November 2015 to December 2025 across its 65 South Australian sites, returning more than $2.6 million to around 1,500 current and former employees. Self-reporting and a clear audit trail were central to that outcome.

**Litigation.** Where an employer disputes a compliance notice, or the FWO pursues a serious contravention through the courts, matters proceed to the Federal Circuit and Family Court or the Federal Court. This is also the pathway for genuine disputes about award interpretation, as seen in Jats Joint Pty Ltd v Fair Work Ombudsman [2025] FCA 743, where a disability provider successfully challenged the FWO's interpretation of sleepover shift penalties.

**The reverse onus of proof.** This is the detail most directors underestimate. Where an employer has failed to meet record-keeping or payslip obligations and cannot provide a reasonable excuse, the onus shifts: the employer must disprove the underpayment allegation, rather than the FWO proving it occurred. Poor records do not just make an audit harder. They can flip the legal burden onto you.

## The 7 highest-risk SCHADS clauses to audit first

### 1. Classification and pay point accuracy

SCHADS has [four streams and up to eight classification levels](https://tesseractapps.com.au/blog/schads-award-2026-the-complete-guide-for-ndis-and-disability-providers), and the gap between them is material. A Level 2 worker in the Home Care and Disability stream sits on a different base rate to a Level 2 SACS stream worker, and some providers compound the risk by classifying support workers under the Home Care Award instead of SCHADS entirely.

**Audit query:** For a sample of 10 to 15 employees, does the assigned stream and level match their actual duties, independent of what payroll currently shows?

### 2. Sleepover and night shift loadings

This is the most contested area of the award, with the rules changing three times in under two years. The FWO originally treated work either side of a sleepover as one continuous shift, attracting a 15% loading. The Federal Court rejected that in Jats Joint, and the Full Federal Court dismissed the FWO's appeal in March 2026. The Fair Work Commission then varied the award itself: from the first full pay period on or after 1 June 2026, sleepovers again do not count as a break.

**Audit query:** For every [sleepover](https://tesseractapps.com.au/blog/schads-sleepover-active-hours-travel) shift in the last 12 months, which rule set was applied, and does it match the legal position in force on that pay date?

### 3. Travel, transport, and fare allowances

These depend on [shift-by-shift detail](https://tesseractapps.com.au/blog/how-ndis-providers-track-employee-hours-accurately), client location, distance, and mode of travel, none of which a flat rate captures. This was central to the Agape matter, where a casual employee's underpayment included travel allowances alongside their minimum hourly rate.

**Audit query:** Do paid travel allowances match actual rostered travel distances, checked against roster data rather than payroll's own calculation?

### 4. NES entitlements alongside the award

SCHADS breaches rarely appear alone. In the Agape matter, the same investigation that found allowance shortfalls also found a full-time employee underpaid annual leave under the NES.

**Audit query:** For long-tenured employees, does accrued and paid leave match hours worked, including any leave periods that affect accrual?

### 5. Broken shifts and minimum engagement

[Broken shifts](https://tesseractapps.com.au/blog/schads-broken-shifts-rostering-without-breaches) and [casual minimum engagement](https://tesseractapps.com.au/blog/schads-minimum-engagement) periods are technically dense and easy to miscalculate manually. Errors rarely look dramatic on one payslip but compound fast across a large casual workforce.

**Audit query:** Were minimum engagement periods applied regardless of actual hours worked, and were broken shift allowances paid where the structure meets the award's definition?

### 6. Annual Wage Review timing

SCHADS rates increase from the first full pay period on or after 1 July each year. Rates rose 3.5% from 1 July 2025, with a further 4.75% increase from 1 July 2026. Providers relying on manual updates routinely miss the exact pay period boundary.

**Audit query:** For the pay period following each of the last three 1 July dates, were rates updated from the correct pay period, not the calendar date?

### 7. Record-keeping completeness

Employers must keep employee records for seven years and issue payslips within one working day of payment, legible, in English, and readily accessible on request.

**Audit query:** Can you produce, within 24 hours, seven years of payslips, timesheets, and classification history for any nominated employee? If the honest answer involves scattered systems or a former staff member's memory, that is your highest-priority fix, regardless of what the underlying pay data shows.

## Building your evidence pack

An evidence pack is the structured proof that your interpretation of SCHADS was reasonable, applied consistently, and can be reconstructed on request. At minimum, each employee file should produce:

- **Classification history,** including stream, level, and pay point at each point in employment, with reasoning for any changes.
- **Roster and timesheet records,** showing rostered versus actual hours and any sleepover, broken shift, or overtime components.
- **Allowance calculations,** showing how travel and other allowances were derived from actual shift data.
- **Leave accrual records,** reconciled against the NES, not just the award.
- **Payslips,** issued within one working day and containing everything required under the Fair Work Regulations.
- **A change log,** documenting when rate updates and interpretation changes, such as the June 2026 sleepover determination, were applied and from which pay period.

The organisations that come out of an FWO matter best, Uniting Communities among them, are the ones that can produce this kind of structured, dated evidence quickly. The ones that struggle are reconstructing history from memory and disconnected spreadsheets under time pressure.

## Running the self-audit

1. **Start with record-keeping, not pay accuracy.** Without clean records you cannot reliably audit anything else, and you are carrying the reverse onus risk regardless.
2. **Work through the seven clauses above in order,** using a meaningful sample rather than your whole workforce.
3. **Cross-reference against the NES, not just the award.** Leave and notice entitlements are just as enforceable.
4. **Quantify what you find.** A dollar figure and affected employee count turn a vague concern into an actionable plan.
5. **Decide on self-reporting early, with legal advice.** The Uniting Communities outcome shows early, transparent self-reporting materially changes how the FWO responds.
6. **Fix the system, not just the back pay.** A remediation payment addresses the past; only a change to [how rosters are built](https://tesseractapps.com.au/blog/how-ndis-providers-track-employee-hours-accurately) prevents the same clause failing again next quarter.

## Why manual audits struggle to keep up?

SCHADS changes more often than most modern awards. The sleepover rules alone applied three different legal positions within roughly eighteen months, each requiring different payroll treatment for the same shift type. A spreadsheet-based audit answers whether you were compliant on the day you checked. It cannot confirm that every pay period since your last audit applied the version of the rules actually in force at the time. Ongoing compliance needs [classification, allowance, and shift-penalty logic](https://tesseractapps.com.au/blog/schads-award-breaches-fair-work-software-prevention) checked at the moment each shift is built and paid, not reconstructed months later.
